The Most Critical Step in IMAT Prep Isn't Studying - It's Planning

Maya Oct 19, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: planning matters more than raw effort, because it's what decides whether your hours are actually aimed at what gets tested, or just feel productive while quietly missing the mark. Most candidates skip this step entirely and go straight to opening a textbook on day one.

That's backwards. Before you've decided what "ready" even looks like for you, specifically, more studying doesn't compound into a better outcome, it just compounds into more hours spent without a clear target.

Why studying without a plan doesn't work the way it feels like it should

Putting in hours feels like progress, so it's easy to mistake activity for direction. But the IMAT isn't tested evenly. Some topics show up every year, some show up once, and some you could skip entirely and barely notice the difference in your score. Without a plan built around that reality, you end up studying whatever's in front of you next, in whatever order a book happens to present it, instead of what actually moves your result.

The same logic extends past the exam itself. Studying medicine in Italy is its own multi-year decision, which university fits your profile, what the first year actually demands, how the system differs from what you're used to, and getting that picture wrong doesn't cost you a bad week, it costs you a bad year.

Why this isn't something to figure out alone, by trial and error

Here's the part most candidates get wrong: they treat planning as something they should be able to work out themselves, through enough Googling and enough forum threads. But planning well requires exactly the kind of judgment that only comes from having actually been through the process, or having worked closely with people who have. A parent who's navigated a competitive admissions process before, an older sibling or friend a year or two ahead, a colleague who's already made the Italy decision, or a mentor who scored well on the exam itself, all of them can see patterns and pitfalls that are genuinely invisible the first time through.

This isn't about needing permission to start. It's about not wasting your first real attempt at planning on guesses, when the people around you who've actually done this could save you months of drift for the cost of one honest conversation.

What a real plan actually looks like

A real plan isn't a color-coded calendar. It's a small number of decisions made early and made well: which sections of the exam are worth the most of your time, which universities are realistic targets for your profile, and roughly how your months should be split between learning content and practicing under real exam conditions. Everything else, the actual weekly schedule, adjusts around those decisions. Get the big calls right early, and the day-to-day planning becomes simple. Get them wrong, and no amount of daily discipline fixes a plan aimed at the wrong target.

This is exactly what mentorship is built around. Not more material to study, but direct input from someone who's already done this, so your plan is built on experience instead of guesswork from day one.

Apply for the mentorship

You don't have to figure this out alone, and honestly, you shouldn't try to. Talk to your parents, talk to people a step ahead of you, talk to someone who's actually scored well on this exam. The plan you build from that input will beat the one you build from guessing, every time.
