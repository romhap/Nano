Why 92% of IMAT Candidates Fail (And It's Not What You Think)

Rom Aug 24, 2026 · 4 min read

Every year, roughly 12,000 people sit the IMAT. About 900 get a seat. That means 92% walk away with nothing — and having sat in lecture halls with a lot of them, I can tell you almost none of them failed because they weren't smart enough.

It's rarely a knowledge problem

Most candidates who don't get in actually know a lot of biology, chemistry, and physics. The gap usually isn't raw knowledge — it's exam-specific execution. Knowing a topic in isolation and being able to deploy it correctly, under time pressure, in the exact multiple-choice format the IMAT uses, with a real penalty for wrong answers, are two different skills. Most people only ever train the first one.

The three silent killers

1. Studying broad instead of studying by weight. Biology is 23 of 60 questions. Physics & Maths is 13. Most candidates split their study hours roughly 50/50 between subjects they find equally intimidating, instead of matching their hours to how much of the actual exam each subject represents. That mismatch alone can cost more points than any single knowledge gap.

2. Never simulating the clock. A huge number of candidates prepare entirely through untimed practice — reading, reviewing, doing a few questions at their own pace — and the first time they feel real 100-minute pressure is on exam day itself. That's the worst possible moment to discover how you perform under time stress.

3. Following a generic plan built for an average candidate who doesn't exist. Most prep courses hand every student the same syllabus, the same pacing, the same practice sets — regardless of whether your actual weak point is chemistry, timing, or reading comprehension. A plan built for "the average candidate" is, by definition, wrong for almost everyone individually.

What the top scorers actually do differently

We've said this before and it's still true: the top scorers since 2011 have never really changed their approach. They study the syllabus by actual weight, not by what feels urgent. They simulate real timed conditions early, not the week before. And they build a plan around their own specific weak points instead of a generic one built for someone else's.

None of that requires more raw hours than everyone else is already putting in. It requires spending those hours differently — which is a far less comfortable thing to hear than "just study harder," but it's the actual difference between the 8% and the 92%.
