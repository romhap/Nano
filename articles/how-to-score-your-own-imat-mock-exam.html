How to Score Your Own IMAT Mock Exam Without Fooling Yourself

Rom Aug 30, 2026 · 4 min read

Every mock score gets treated as a fact once it's written down, but a self-marked mock is only as accurate as the marking itself. Nobody double-checks the marking. The number just gets copied into a spreadsheet and trusted, and that's exactly where a surprising amount of drift creeps in.

Marking your own paper is not a neutral act

When someone else marks a test, there's no incentive either way. When you mark your own, there's a quiet pull toward generosity on anything ambiguous, especially after a long practice session when the temptation is to just get a number and move on. That pull doesn't feel like cheating. It feels like giving yourself the benefit of the doubt on a question that was "basically right." Multiplied across forty or fifty borderline calls a month, it adds up to a score that's flattering rather than accurate.

The three habits that quietly inflate the number

The first is leniency on your own borderline answers, counting a right idea expressed sloppily as a full mark it wouldn't get on the real exam. The second is marking from memory of the answer key instead of checking it fresh each time, which lets you unconsciously round in your own favor. The third, and the most common, is forgetting to apply the actual penalty for wrong answers when tallying by hand. The IMAT scores each question at +1.5 for correct, -0.4 for wrong, and 0 for blank, and a hand count that just adds up "correct minus wrong" without that exact weighting will consistently overstate the total, sometimes by several points.

Why this stays invisible until it matters

A self-marked score that's a few points too generous doesn't feel wrong in the moment, because there's nothing to compare it against. It only becomes visible when a mock scored under real conditions, ideally with someone else checking the key, comes back noticeably lower than the self-marked numbers have been suggesting for weeks. At that point it looks like a sudden drop in performance, when really it's the first accurate number in a run of slightly inflated ones.

How to mark a mock the way it will actually be graded

Use the answer key fresh every time instead of relying on memory, mark question by question rather than skimming for a rough impression, and apply the exact scoring weights rather than a simplified correct-versus-wrong count. If a question is genuinely ambiguous, mark it wrong and move on instead of arguing yourself into a point. A slightly lower, honest score is far more useful than a flattering one, because only the honest one tells you anything true about where the gaps actually are.
