Reading Published IMAT Cutoff Scores by University

Maya Sep 20, 2026 · 4 min read

Every candidate eventually finds a list of last year's cutoff scores by university, and almost every candidate reads it the same wrong way: as a single line to beat. Clear that number and you're in, miss it and you're not. The reality is messier, and if you treat one year's cutoff as a fixed target, you'll either aim too low at a school that just got harder or write off a school that's actually gotten easier to reach.

Why the number moves every year

A university's cutoff isn't set in advance, it's simply where the ranking lands once seats run out, which means it shifts with three things at once: how many seats that university offers that year, how many candidates apply to it specifically, and how the whole applicant pool performs on that year's exam. A school can raise its cutoff purely because it got more popular, with no change to its seats or difficulty at all, and a school can drop several points because one year's exam ran harder across the board. None of that is visible in a single number on its own.

The mistake of aiming at last year's exact score

Treating last year's cutoff as this year's target ignores all of that movement. Aim to just clear it and a small swing in applicant numbers or exam difficulty can put you just under a line that moved without warning. It's the same error as reading one mock exam score as your true level, a single data point standing in for something that actually needs a trend.

What a multi-year trend actually tells you

Pull three or four years of cutoffs for a university you're considering instead of just the most recent one. A cutoff that's climbed steadily tells you the school is getting more competitive and you should build in more margin. One that's bounced around within a tight range tells you it's relatively stable, and one year's number is a fair enough estimate. One that's been falling tells you there may be more room than the newest single figure suggests. The direction and the spread matter more than the exact latest value.

How to actually use this when building your list

Sort your target universities into reach, likely, and safety tiers based on the range a school's cutoff has actually occupied over recent years, with margin built in on either side, not the exact last number. A list built this way survives a harder-than-usual exam year or a sudden spike in applicants far better than one built around beating a single line.
