The IMAT Syllabus Is a Mess — Here's How to Actually Use It

Maya Jul 31, 2026 · 5 min read

The official IMAT syllabus is a government decree. It reads like one, too — dense, formal, and completely unhelpful if what you actually need is a study plan you can follow this week.

What it actually covers

Underneath the formal language, it maps onto the exam's five sections:

  • Biology (23 questions) — cell biology, genetics and heredity, human anatomy & physiology, bioenergetics/metabolism, evolution and ecology.
  • Chemistry (15 questions) — general and inorganic chemistry, atomic structure and bonding, stoichiometry, acids/bases and pH, electrochemistry, organic chemistry basics.
  • Physics & Maths (13 questions) — mechanics and kinematics, thermodynamics, electrostatics and circuits, plus algebra, functions, probability and geometry.
  • Logical Reasoning & Problems (5 questions) — deductive and numerical reasoning, argument analysis.
  • Reading Skills & Knowledge (4 questions) — reading comprehension plus general culture, history and institutions.

Vague on purpose — or at least, vague in practice

Here's the problem: the decree lists topic names, not depth or emphasis. "Genetics" could mean one question about basic Mendelian ratios or something more layered — the syllabus document alone can't tell you which, because it's a scope document, not a weighting document. Two students can both "cover the syllabus" and walk in with completely different levels of actual exam-readiness, because covering a topic name isn't the same as knowing which parts of it get tested.

The only way to find the real weighting is to cross-reference the syllabus against actual past papers — which topics come up every single year, which show up once and never again, and which get tested at a level far shallower than the syllabus wording implies. (I wrote about exactly that here: I Read Every Past IMAT Paper So You Don't Have To.)

How to turn it into an actual plan

Don't study the syllabus top to bottom as written. Instead:

  1. Take each broad topic and break it into concrete, checkable subtopics — "genetics" becomes "Mendelian ratios," "pedigree analysis," "X-linked inheritance," and so on.
  2. Rank those subtopics by how often they actually show up in real papers, not by how important they sound.
  3. Go deep only where the exam actually rewards depth. A subtopic that's worth one question a year doesn't need textbook-chapter mastery — it needs recognition, not expertise.

This is genuinely the most time-expensive part of prepping alone — most candidates either skip it (and study unevenly) or spend weeks doing it by hand before they've answered a single practice question.
