After the IMAT

Didn't Get In? Here's What Actually Changes for Your Retake

Maya Aug 16, 2026 · 4 min read

Not clearing the cutoff on your first attempt feels like the end of something. It isn't. Plenty of students we know - including people who ended up with genuinely strong scores - didn't get in on the first try. What actually determines whether a retake goes differently isn't willpower. It's whether anything about the approach actually changes.

The mistake most retakers make

Doing the same prep again, just for longer, is the single most common retake mistake. If a method didn't get you the score you needed once, doing more of the identical thing rarely closes the gap - it just delays the same result. A retake needs a real diagnosis of what specifically went wrong, not just more hours in the same direction.

Diagnose before you re-plan

Go back through your actual exam performance, section by section. Was the gap concentrated in one or two subjects, or spread evenly? Was it a knowledge problem, or did you know the material but ran out of time, or froze under the real exam-day pressure in a way practice never replicated? These are three completely different fixes, and most first-attempt candidates never isolate which one actually happened to them.

What genuinely tends to change outcomes on a retake

A study plan built around your specific weak points instead of a generic full review of everything. Real timed practice under actual exam conditions, not just untimed content review - many first-time candidates never simulated the real clock until test day itself. And, often the most overlooked piece: an outside perspective on what actually went wrong, since it's genuinely hard to diagnose your own blind spots from inside your own preparation.

Second attempts with a real change in approach tend to move meaningfully. Second attempts that just repeat the first one, only more anxiously, usually don't.

Going into a retake and want a plan built around what actually went wrong last time, not a generic restart? This is exactly the kind of situation 1:1 mentorship is built for.

One attempt not going your way says something about that attempt. It doesn't say anything fixed about you. What you change between now and the next sitting is what actually decides the next result.
