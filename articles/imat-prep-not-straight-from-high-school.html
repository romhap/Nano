Prepping for the IMAT When You're Not Fresh Out of High School

Rom Sep 21, 2026 · 4 min read

Most IMAT content, ours included, quietly assumes a specific candidate: someone finishing high school, sitting the exam the same year, surrounded by classmates doing the exact same thing. If that's not you, a decent chunk of that advice still applies, but a few things genuinely feel different, and it helps to name them instead of pretending they don't exist.

The gap you might feel that has nothing to do with the exam

If you're coming back to structured studying after years away from a classroom, or you're prepping alongside a job, a degree in something unrelated, or other responsibilities, the loudest voices in group chats and forums are usually still 18-year-olds fresh out of school. Their pace, their study hours, their frame of reference for "normal" often doesn't map onto your actual life, and that mismatch can feel like evidence you're behind when it's really just evidence you're solving a different logistical problem.

What's actually different about your prep

Time is usually the real constraint, not raw ability. You likely have fewer uninterrupted hours per week than someone whose only job is finishing school, which means efficiency matters more than volume for you specifically. On the other hand, if you've already been through university-level coursework or years in a job that demanded discipline, you're probably better than average at sticking to a schedule and studying with real focus in shorter blocks, which is exactly the skill a lot of younger candidates are still building.

What genuinely doesn't change

The exam itself doesn't care about your path here. The syllabus is the same, the scoring is the same, and the ranking system treats every applicant's number identically regardless of how they got to test day. There's no separate, harder bar for candidates who took a longer or less linear route into IMAT prep.

The practical adjustment worth making

Build your study plan around your actual weekly hours, not around a generic timeline written for someone with a full school day free. A realistic plan built around, say, ten focused hours a week beats an ambitious plan copied from someone with forty, because the second one just sets you up to feel like you're failing a schedule that was never built for your life in the first place.
