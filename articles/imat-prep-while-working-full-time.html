How to Actually Fit IMAT Prep Around a Full-Time Job

Maya Oct 2, 2026 · 4 min read

A growing number of the candidates we talk to aren't students at all. They're working full-time, sometimes in a field that has nothing to do with medicine, and trying to fit IMAT prep into whatever is left of their week. Most study advice online quietly assumes you have large, movable blocks of free time to work with. If your schedule is a fixed eight or nine hours a day plus a commute, that advice doesn't just need scaling down, it needs rebuilding from a different starting point.

Why this is a different problem, not a smaller one

A student can shift their day around: study in the afternoon instead of the morning, push a session to tomorrow if today falls apart. A full-time job doesn't bend that way. Your working hours are fixed, your energy afterward is genuinely lower, and the hours you do have are a hard constraint rather than a flexible budget. Planning around "I'll find the time" fails here in a way it doesn't for a student, because there usually isn't slack hiding somewhere in the day waiting to be found.

Protect a small number of hours like they're a second job

The candidates who make real progress this way tend to pick one or two fixed windows, often early morning before work or a set block right after it, and defend them the same way they'd defend a shift they're scheduled for. The specific hours matter less than the consistency. A smaller block that happens every single weekday builds more real progress over a few months than an ambitious plan that depends on energy and motivation holding up after a nine-hour workday, which it usually won't.

Let weekends carry the full-length mocks

A proper IMAT mock needs 100 uninterrupted minutes, which almost never exists on a weeknight after work. Instead of trying to force one in, use weekday blocks for content review, flashcards, and section-specific practice in shorter chunks, and reserve weekend mornings specifically for full timed mock exams. This isn't a compromise, it's actually the right division of labor: content review tolerates being broken into small pieces far better than exam-condition practice does.

Stop measuring yourself against a student's hours

The fastest way to talk yourself out of this plan is to compare your weekly study hours to candidates who are full-time students with nothing else on their plate. That comparison is measuring two different situations with the same ruler. Track your own trend across mock scores and section accuracy instead of total hours logged, because consistent, well-protected hours against a fixed schedule can move a score just as much as a larger but less consistent block of "free" time ever does.

Building a prep plan that actually survives contact with a real job and a real schedule, instead of one that looks good on paper and falls apart by week three, is exactly what 1:1 mentorship helps you get right.

Apply for the mentorship

Working full-time doesn't rule out a strong IMAT score, but it does rule out copying a schedule built for someone with an entirely different day. Build the plan around the hours you actually have, protect them consistently, and let your own trend, not someone else's hour count, tell you whether it's working.
