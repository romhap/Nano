What to Cut When You Only Have Six Weeks to Prepare for the IMAT

Rom Oct 1, 2026 · 4 min read

Every year, some candidates realize how little time is actually left with around six weeks on the clock, whether that's because they committed to the IMAT late or because life simply ate the months they'd planned to use. The instinct is to try to cover the full syllabus anyway, just faster. That's the wrong goal. Six weeks isn't a compressed version of a six-month plan, it's a different problem with a different answer.

What six weeks is actually good for

It's genuinely enough time to move your score, sometimes by a meaningful amount. It is not enough time to turn weak sections into strong ones from a standing start, and treating it like it is means spreading your remaining hours thin enough that nothing gets covered well. The goal in a short runway isn't "learn everything." It's "walk in scoring as high as this amount of time can realistically produce," which is a narrower and much more achievable target.

Triage by section weight first, not by comfort

With limited hours, the instinct is to spend them on the section that feels most fixable or most familiar. Resist that. Start from how much each section actually weighs in your final score, and put your remaining hours where the weight is, even if that section feels less rewarding to study than one you already half-know. A few extra points recovered in a heavily-weighted section does more for your overall score than the same hours spent polishing a section that barely moves it.

Inside each section, cut topics you can't realistically learn from zero

Once you know where the hours are going, be honest about what's learnable in six weeks and what isn't. A topic that takes real time to build from scratch, a multi-step chemistry mechanism, a physics derivation you've never seen, isn't a good bet this late. Topics that show up on the exam most years and can be learned to a "good enough to answer correctly" level quickly are a much better use of the same hours. That means accepting, on purpose, that a handful of questions will likely get guessed on test day. That's a deliberate tradeoff, not a failure of the plan.

The one thing that doesn't get cut

Whatever else shrinks, keep at least one timed, full-length mock exam going every week or two through the whole six weeks. Pacing and composure under a ticking clock are a trained skill, not content, and they fade fast if you stop practicing them to free up more review time. Cutting a mock to "save time for studying" usually costs more score than it saves, because it's cutting the one part of your prep that a short runway can't afford to re-learn in the final days.

Deciding what to cut and what to protect with a limited runway is exactly the kind of call that's hard to make objectively about your own prep. Getting a second, experienced opinion on that triage is a big part of what 1:1 mentorship is for.

Apply for the mentorship

Six weeks won't feel like enough time, and in one sense it isn't, since there's always more syllabus than hours. But a deliberately triaged six weeks consistently outperforms a frantic, evenly-spread one. The cuts aren't a compromise on the plan. For a short runway, they are the plan.
