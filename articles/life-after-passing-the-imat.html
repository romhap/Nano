Life As A Student

What Nobody Tells You About Life After You Pass the IMAT

Maya Sep 4, 2026 · 4 min read

Everyone obsesses over passing the IMAT — understandably, it's the gate everything else sits behind. Almost nobody talks about what happens on the other side of that gate. I'm four years in now, so here's the version I wish someone had told me before I got my results.

Getting in is a start, not a finish line

The exam decides whether you get a seat. It doesn't prepare you for what the seat actually involves — moving to a new country, often alone, into a demanding six-year programme taught in English inside a country where daily life mostly isn't. That adjustment is its own project, separate from anything the IMAT tested.

The early years are heavy, in a different way

The first couple of years are lecture-dense — anatomy, physiology, biochemistry, at a depth and pace well beyond anything the IMAT touched. It's not harder in the "trick question" sense the exam sometimes felt like. It's harder in volume and consistency: showing up and absorbing a lot of material, every week, for years, with no single exam to rehearse against — just an ongoing standard to keep meeting.

Clinical rotations change everything

At a certain point, lecture halls give way to hospital wards — tirocini, clinical rotations where you're actually present for patient care under supervision. That's usually where it stops feeling theoretical and starts feeling like the actual reason you did all of this. It's also where the gap between "knew it for an exam" and "can apply it in front of a real patient" becomes very obvious, very fast.

Community matters more than people expect

Studying medicine abroad, in a language and culture that isn't fully your own, is genuinely isolating if you let it be. The students who do best long-term tend to be the ones who build a real support network early — classmates, seniors, mentors — rather than trying to power through years of dense material entirely alone.

This is part of why mentorship doesn't stop at the exam. Rom and I both walk students through what's ahead, not just how to pass — because passing is the beginning of the actual work, not the end of it.

Apply for the mentorship

Pass the exam because it's genuinely worth passing. Just don't walk in thinking the hard part ends there — it's the door, not the destination.
