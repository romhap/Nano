What to Do If You Panic Halfway Through the IMAT

Maya Sep 11, 2026 · 4 min read

It happens to almost everyone at some point during the IMAT, usually without warning. You hit a question that doesn't make sense, or you glance at the clock and realize you're behind, and suddenly your mind goes blank on things you knew perfectly well an hour earlier. That moment isn't a sign your prep failed. It's a physical response, and like any physical response, there are specific things that make it worse and specific things that actually pull you out of it.

What it actually feels like

It rarely starts with the hardest question in the section. More often it starts with a question that's just slightly unfamiliar, one that doesn't match the pattern you drilled, and the surprise of that is what triggers the spiral. Your reading speed drops, you reread the same sentence three times without absorbing it, and the questions after it start to feel harder too, even when they aren't. The content knowledge hasn't gone anywhere. What's gone, temporarily, is your ability to access it calmly.

The three things that make it worse

Staying on the question that triggered it is the biggest one. Every extra second spent trying to force an answer out of a foggy head reinforces the panic instead of resolving it. Doing the math on how many questions are left and how little time remains is the second, because it turns a manageable situation into an imagined catastrophe before you've actually lost anything. And treating the moment as evidence that you're not ready is the third, since that thought alone can do more damage to your remaining performance than the original hard question ever could.

What actually works in the moment

Mark your best guess, move to the next question, and give yourself permission to come back later if time allows. The physical act of moving on, rather than sitting with the discomfort, is what actually breaks the spiral, not willpower or telling yourself to calm down. A few slow breaths while you read the next question's stem also helps more than it sounds like it should, because panic tightens your breathing without you noticing, and that shallow breathing is part of what's keeping the fog going. The goal isn't to eliminate the feeling instantly. It's to stop it from bleeding into the next five questions.

Building the habit before test day

This is much easier to do on exam day if you've already rehearsed it. During mock exams, deliberately practice the skip-and-return move on at least one question per section, even ones you could probably solve if you pushed through, just so the motion feels automatic rather than like an admission of failure. Candidates who treat skipping as a normal, planned part of their strategy recover from a rough patch far faster than those who've only ever treated it as a last resort.

Still not sure whether your exam-day nerves are about the material or about not trusting your plan? Working out which one it actually is, and fixing the right one, is exactly the kind of thing 1:1 mentorship helps with.
