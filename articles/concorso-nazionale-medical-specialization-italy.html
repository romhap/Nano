The Concorso Nazionale: The Exam That Decides Your Specialty After Italian Med School

Rom Nov 1, 2026 · 4 min read

Graduating Italian medical school and passing the Esame di Stato licenses you to practice generally, but it doesn't hand you a specialty. A separate national, merit-ranked exam, the concorso, decides which specialization school you actually enter, and because seats per specialty and per city are limited, your rank on that exam matters as much as your preference does.

Candidates sitting the IMAT are, understandably, not thinking six years ahead. But it's worth knowing early that the six years of medical school aren't the last competitive step in this system. There's another one waiting on the other side, and it works on a logic candidates have already lived through once: a ranked list, limited seats, and a result that depends on both.

Why finishing the degree isn't the finish line

The Esame di Stato licenses you as a general physician once you've finished your coursework and thesis. That's a real, meaningful milestone. But practicing as a cardiologist, a surgeon, or a psychiatrist requires years of further training inside a scuola di specializzazione, and admission to one of those schools isn't automatic just because you have a degree and a license. It's awarded through a separate, competitive national process.

What the concorso actually decides

The concorso ranks every applicant nationally based on exam performance. Each specialty, in each region and teaching hospital, has a fixed number of seats that year. Once the ranking is published, seats are assigned top-down: the highest-ranked candidates get first pick from whichever specialties and locations still have openings, and the list works its way down from there. Your preference order matters, but only within what your rank can actually reach.

Why seats and location are both limited

This is the part that surprises people who assumed "get the degree, pick your specialty" was how it worked. Popular specialties in major cities fill from the top of the ranking almost immediately. A strong rank gets you real choice. A middling one might mean a specialty you're fine with but didn't picture, in a city you didn't plan around, or a wait for the next round. None of that reflects whether you were a good medical student, it reflects where the national ranking and that year's seat count happened to intersect.

What's actually worth doing about this, years in advance

Nothing about this needs to change how you study for the IMAT today. But it's worth using your eventual clinical years and rotations as genuine information-gathering rather than a box to check, so that by the time the concorso comes around, your preference list reflects specialties you've actually seen up close, not ones you imagined from the outside. Flexibility about location, in particular, tends to matter more in practice than most students expect going in.

Thinking this far ahead is exactly the kind of long-view question 1:1 mentorship is built for. Not the concorso itself, that's years away, but the university and course decisions you're making now that quietly shape your options later.
