Does Already Having a Bachelor's Degree Change Your IMAT Application?

Maya Sep 23, 2026 · 4 min read

We hear from a fair number of candidates who already finished a bachelor's degree, sometimes in a completely unrelated field, and want to know if that changes how the IMAT works for them. The short answer is: less than they expect, and not in the way they usually assume.

It's a reasonable thing to wonder. Most other paths into higher education give you some kind of credit or advantage for prior study. Medicine in Italy, entered through the IMAT, doesn't work that way, and knowing that early saves people from planning around an advantage that isn't there.

The IMAT doesn't have a graduate-entry track

Medicine and Surgery in Italy is a six-year, single-cycle degree that starts from year one for every admitted student, regardless of what they studied before. There's no accelerated or graduate-entry version of the program the way some countries offer for candidates who already hold a degree. Whether you're arriving straight from high school or ten years into an unrelated career, you take the same IMAT, compete on the same ranking list, and if admitted, start the same first year alongside everyone else.

What your existing degree does and doesn't do for you

It doesn't shorten the program, and it doesn't exempt you from any first-year courses, even ones that overlap heavily with what you already studied. A candidate with a biology or chemistry degree still sits through introductory biology and chemistry again in year one. What it can do is make certain content genuinely easier to relearn quickly, since you're refreshing material rather than meeting it for the first time, and it can sharpen the study habits and exam stamina you bring into IMAT prep. Neither of those shows up as a formal advantage on the application itself, but both can show up in how efficiently you prepare.

Why some candidates in this position hesitate anyway

The hesitation usually isn't really about the IMAT itself, it's about the six years that follow it. Starting a full six-year program after already completing a degree feels like a bigger commitment than it does for an eighteen-year-old, and that feeling is legitimate. But it's worth separating two different questions: whether you're eligible and competitive for the IMAT, which a prior degree doesn't change one way or the other, and whether starting medicine from scratch at this point in your life is the right call for you, which is a personal decision, not an eligibility question.

How to actually decide if it's worth it

Treat your existing degree as a separate factor from your IMAT prep, not a shortcut through it. Prepare exactly as thoroughly as any other candidate would, since the exam doesn't grade on background. Then weigh the six-year commitment on its own terms: what you'd be leaving behind, what you'd be building toward, and whether medicine specifically, not just "a different career," is the thing pulling you back. Candidates who separate those two questions tend to make a clearer decision than those who let one blur into the other.
