Why Treating the IMAT as Your Only Option Makes Prep Harder

Rom Oct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

A lot of candidates quietly decide, somewhere in the first few weeks of prep, that the IMAT is the only path they're willing to accept. No other program, no gap year, no alternative route. It feels like commitment. In practice, it usually makes the next several months of studying noticeably harder than they need to be.

What "no backup plan" actually does to your prep

When one exam carries the entire weight of your future, every mock stops being a data point and starts being a referendum on whether that future is still possible. A single bad section score on a Tuesday afternoon can spiral into hours lost to catastrophizing instead of reviewing the mistakes that caused it. The pressure doesn't make you study better, it just makes every setback feel bigger than the information in it actually warrants.

It also distorts how you make smaller decisions

All-in thinking doesn't stay contained to the big picture. It leaks into daily choices too, pushing people to skip rest days they need, avoid mock exams they're scared to take, or keep re-reading the same chapter because stopping feels like giving something up. The decisions stop being about what actually improves your score and start being about managing anxiety in the moment, which are not the same thing.

A real Plan B doesn't compete with Plan A

The candidates who handle the pressure best aren't the ones who care less about getting in. They're the ones who've genuinely thought through what happens if this specific attempt doesn't work, whether that's a retake, a different country's medicine program, or a related degree for a year, and then put that thought away. Having an answer to "what if not" doesn't lower your effort on "what if yes." It just removes the catastrophe from the equation, so a bad mock is bad information again instead of a referendum.

Building one without it becoming a distraction

This isn't a call to spend real study hours researching alternate programs. It's closer to a single honest conversation with yourself, or with someone you trust, that answers the question once and lets you stop re-litigating it every time a practice score dips. Write down the actual answer if it helps. Then go back to studying for the exam you're actually sitting, with one less thing quietly running in the background.
