Past Papers

I Read Every Past IMAT Paper So You Don't Have To

Maya Jul 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Before my own exam, I did something almost nobody bothers doing properly: I sat down with every past paper I could find, laid them out side by side, and actually studied the pattern — not just the content. Not "did this question", but "why is this question shaped this way, and does that shape repeat."

It does. Here's what years of real papers actually show once you stop reading them one at a time.

The format is far more stable than people assume

Every cycle, someone panics online that "the format changed" or "this year is completely different." When you actually line up multiple recent years question-by-question, the structure barely moves: 60 questions, 100 minutes, five options each, and the same section split — Reading Skills & Knowledge (4 questions), Logical Reasoning & Problems (5), Biology (23), Chemistry (15), Physics & Mathematics (13). That's not a rumor, that's what's actually printed on the papers, year after year, in that exact order.

Knowing the shape of the exam removes an entire category of anxiety. You're not walking in blind to "whatever they throw at you this year." You're walking into a structure you've already rehearsed, because it barely changes.

What the questions actually look like

Reading a real paper front to back teaches you things no summary can:

  • Reading section: a mix of short trivia-style recall ("who wrote X," "which war was between X and Y") and one or two genuine short-passage questions — a real excerpt from journalism, literature, or even a medicine leaflet, followed by "which of these CANNOT be inferred."
  • Logic section: short, self-contained puzzles you should be solving in under a minute — propositional logic, seating arrangements, probability, percentages. Not the long, multi-paragraph word problems people assume from generic "logical reasoning" prep books.
  • Biology, Chemistry, Physics & Maths: direct, clean-number questions. Definitions and mechanisms in biology, formula application with tidy values in chemistry and physics. Not olympiad-hard, not textbook-trivial — calibrated right in between.

And the wrong answers aren't random noise. They're built from real misconceptions — an almost-right definition, a calculation that's one sign or one step off. Once you've seen enough of them, you start recognizing a wrong answer's "shape" almost on instinct.

The real lesson: past papers aren't practice, they're the syllabus

Most people treat past papers as something to save for the final week — a "test yourself" exercise after the real studying is done. That's backwards. The official syllabus lists dozens of topics per subject with no indication of weight. Papers tell you, directly, which dozen mechanisms actually get tested year after year, in what proportion, and in what style.

Reading five years of real chemistry sections taught me more about what's actually worth memorizing than any textbook chapter list did — because a textbook doesn't know what the exam cares about. The exam does. It just doesn't say so out loud. You have to go find the pattern yourself.
