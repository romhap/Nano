How the IMAT Waitlist Actually Moves After Results Come Out

Maya Oct 6, 2026 · 4 min read

Results come out, you check your rank against the number you'd been hoping to clear, and it feels like the story is over, either you got the seat or you didn't. It isn't over. The national ranking list keeps moving for weeks after the first publication, and a huge number of candidates who don't clear their top choice on day one end up there a few rounds later without doing anything differently at all.

Why the list moves even though nobody retakes the exam

Your score is fixed the moment results publish. What isn't fixed is who ends up sitting in which seat. Every candidate ranked above you has also listed several universities in order of preference, and when one of them is placed into a school and confirms it, every seat they didn't take at a university further down their own list becomes free again. That freed seat gets offered to the next candidate on that university's ranking, who may in turn vacate a seat somewhere else. One confirmation can set off several more behind it.

What actually causes the movement

Three things drive it. Candidates placed into their first choice stop needing any other seat they were holding. Candidates who decline a seat outright, because they chose a different country or program, free it immediately. And candidates who miss a confirmation deadline get removed from that seat automatically, which also frees it. None of this has anything to do with your own score. It's entirely about what people ranked above you choose to do with theirs.

How many rounds this takes, and where it slows down

Movement happens in official rounds published over several weeks, not continuously. The first one or two rounds usually carry the biggest shifts, since that's when most candidates who cleared a strong first choice confirm and vacate everything else. After that, movement gets smaller and slower round by round, as the remaining open seats sit lower on more people's lists and take longer to get claimed. A rank that looks out of reach in round one can be well within reach by round three or four, especially for universities that weren't anyone's obvious top pick.

What to actually do while it's happening

Don't treat the first published list as your final answer, and don't assume a seat you're currently sitting in is guaranteed either, confirmation deadlines are real and missing one costs you the spot. Check each new round as it's published rather than guessing where you'll land, and keep your full preference list in mind rather than fixating on just the one university you're currently tracking. The list rewards patience through several rounds far more than it rewards reacting to the very first number you see.
