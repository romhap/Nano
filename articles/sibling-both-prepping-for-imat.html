What Happens When You and a Sibling Are Both Prepping for the IMAT

Maya Sep 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Studying for the IMAT alongside a friend has its own strain, but you go home at the end of the day and it stays at the library. Studying for it alongside a sibling is different, because there's no "at the end of the day" - you're at the same dinner table, your parents ask both of you how the mock exam went in the same breath, and every score either of you mentions gets compared whether you meant it to be or not.

Why it feels different from any other kind of comparison

With a friend, you choose how much to share. With a sibling under the same roof, the information leaks out anyway - through a closed bedroom door, an overheard phone call, a parent mentioning "your brother scored X this week." You didn't sign up to compete, but the comparison happens by default, and it happens whether the two of you are close or barely speak. That's the part that makes it heavier than ordinary peer comparison: you can't opt out of the data.

The trap of syncing your pace to theirs

Because you're studying the same syllabus at the same time, it's tempting to assume you should be moving at the same speed - starting mock exams together, hitting the same topics the same week, feeling behind if they finish a section review before you do. But two people rarely learn a subject at the same rate, even with identical material and identical effort. One of you may find chemistry intuitive and burn through it quickly; the other may need real time there and move faster through biology instead. Forcing a shared timeline onto two different learning curves usually just makes the slower-on-this-topic sibling feel behind for no real reason.

What's worth sharing, and what isn't

Splitting logistics is genuinely efficient - trading notes, sharing a mock exam bank, taking turns finding good practice material. Splitting your actual score numbers, on the other hand, rarely helps either of you. A mock score is meant to guide your own next study decision, not to be a running scoreboard the household tracks. It's fine to keep your week-to-week numbers between you and whoever you actually study with in a useful way - a study partner, a mentor, yourself - rather than announcing them at dinner by habit.

If a real gap opens up near the end

Sometimes the outcomes genuinely diverge - one of you is tracking toward an offer, the other toward a retake. That's not a verdict on effort or on which of you tried harder; two people with the same last name and the same kitchen table can still land in different places on the same exam, the same way two candidates from the same school do. Treating a sibling's result as a referendum on your own chances, in either direction, tends to distort your judgment about what you actually still need to work on.

Trying to figure out what you specifically still need to fix, instead of measuring yourself against what's happening across the table? That kind of individual calibration is exactly what 1:1 mentorship is built around.

Apply for the mentorship

You didn't choose to take this exam next to someone whose scores you'll hear about no matter what. But you can choose how much weight you give that information, and your own prep should still be built around your own gaps, not a running comparison you never agreed to keep.
