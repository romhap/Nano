How Much Physiology Actually Shows Up in the IMAT Biology Section

Maya Oct 25, 2026 · 4 min read

Physiology is one of the biggest recurring themes inside the IMAT's Biology section, but it's tested as organ-system mechanism, not anatomy labeling. Biology makes up 23 of the IMAT's 60 questions, and within that, physiology questions ask how a system responds when something changes, not whether you can name a structure on a diagram.

Candidates often treat physiology as the part of Biology where you memorize how each organ system works, system by system, the way a textbook chapter lays it out. That gets you partway there. The exam isn't asking "what does the nephron do," it's asking "if filtration pressure drops, what happens to urine output," and that second question needs you to actually understand the mechanism, not just recite it.

Where physiology sits inside Biology

Human anatomy and physiology is one of the handful of themes that repeat inside Biology every year, alongside cell biology, molecular biology, genetics, bioenergetics and metabolism, and evolution. It doesn't get its own time block or question count, it's woven through Biology's footprint the same way genetics and biochemistry are. Given how many organ systems exist, it's also one of the themes most likely to feel unmanageably large if you approach it as a full anatomy course instead of a focused slice.

Why it feels bigger than it actually needs to be

There are a lot of organ systems, and a full physiology course covers each one in real depth, which is exactly why candidates either try to cram all of it or give up and skip it. Neither is necessary. The IMAT consistently tests physiology at the level of "what does this system do, and what happens to it when one variable changes," not at the depth of a pre-clinical physiology exam. The breadth is wide but the depth per topic is shallow and predictable.

What actually gets asked

Questions cluster around a handful of systems: circulatory (how heart rate, stroke volume, and blood pressure relate to each other), respiratory (gas exchange and how breathing rate responds to blood gas changes), renal (how the nephron filters, reabsorbs, and concentrates urine), nervous (how a signal travels along a neuron and across a synapse), and endocrine (hormone feedback loops, like what happens to a hormone's level when its target process is disrupted). Almost all of them are framed as cause-and-effect: given one change, what else in the system changes as a result.

The skill underneath it is tracing mechanism, not recalling structure

Once you know what a system does, the exam is really testing whether you can follow a chain of cause and effect through it under time pressure. If blood pressure drops, what does the body do to raise it, and which organs and hormones are involved in that response, in what order? That's a tracing skill, not a labeling skill, and it's the same skill tested across every organ system, which means getting good at tracing one feedback loop makes the next one easier, not harder.

How to actually train this

For each major system, build a simple cause-and-effect map: what triggers a change, what senses it, what responds, and what the end result is. Then work real past-paper questions against that map rather than re-reading a physiology textbook chapter by chapter. When you miss one, check whether you had the mechanism wrong or just misread which direction the question was asking about, because those are different problems with different fixes.
