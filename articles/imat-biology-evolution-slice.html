How Much Evolution Actually Shows Up in the IMAT Biology Section

Maya Nov 8, 2026 · 4 min read

Evolution is a smaller, steadier theme inside the IMAT's Biology section, and it's tested through population-level reasoning, not a timeline of species to memorize. Biology makes up 23 of the IMAT's 60 questions, and within that, evolution questions usually describe a population and a selective pressure, then ask what happens to a trait over time, not what happened historically to which species.

Candidates who studied evolution in school often picture it as a history unit: who proposed what, in what order, illustrated with a parade of textbook examples. That's not what gets tested. The exam isn't asking you to recount the history of a theory, it's asking you to apply the logic of selection to a scenario you've never seen before.

Where evolution sits inside Biology

Evolution is one of the handful of themes that repeat inside Biology every year, alongside cell biology, molecular biology, genetics, and bioenergetics and metabolism. It doesn't get its own time block or fixed question count, and compared to some of those other themes it tends to show up more lightly and more predictably, which makes it one of the better-contained parts of Biology to prepare for once you know what the questions are actually built around.

Why it feels vaguer than it actually is

Evolution can feel harder to pin down than a system of the body or a chapter of genetics, because it doesn't come with the same obvious structure to study from. That vagueness usually comes from treating it as a pile of facts and vocabulary, natural selection, adaptation, fitness, speciation, rather than as one underlying mechanism applied to different situations. Once you see it as a single piece of logic, it stops feeling like an open-ended topic and starts feeling like a skill you can actually practice.

What actually gets asked

Questions tend to cluster around a few recurring setups: a population exposed to a new selective pressure, where you're asked whether a trait will become more or less common over time, and why; a comparison of two populations or environments, where you're asked to identify which trait would be favored in each; or a simple diagram or tree, where you're asked to read a branching relationship rather than recall it from memory. Almost all of them are framed as cause-and-effect, given a change in the environment, what happens to a trait's frequency in the population, and why.

The skill underneath it is applying selection logic, not recalling history

Once you know the underlying mechanism, selective pressure acts on existing variation, and traits that improve survival or reproduction become more common over generations, the exam is really testing whether you can apply that same logic to an unfamiliar scenario under time pressure. That's a transferable reasoning skill, not a memorization task, which means getting comfortable with a handful of worked examples makes the next unfamiliar scenario easier, not harder.

How to actually train this

Rather than re-reading a chapter of evolutionary biology, work through real past-paper questions and, for each one, write out in your own words what the selective pressure is, what trait it's acting on, and what the expected outcome is. When you miss one, check whether you misunderstood the selective pressure in the scenario or just misread which direction the question was asking about, because those are different problems with different fixes.

Not sure whether you're quietly over-studying one slice of Biology while under-studying the sections that carry more weight? That kind of misallocated study time is exactly what 1:1 mentorship catches early.

Apply for the mentorship

Evolution rewards candidates who can apply one piece of logic to a new scenario, not candidates who've memorized the most vocabulary. Treat it as a reasoning skill rather than a history unit, and it becomes one of the more predictable parts of Biology to walk into on test day.
