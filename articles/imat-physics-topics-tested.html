The Physics Topics That Actually Show Up on the IMAT (And the Ones That Don't)

Rom Sep 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Physics & Maths is 13 questions total, so the physics half is really only 6 or 7 questions on the whole exam. Yet a lot of candidates prepare for it as if it were a full physics course, working through a textbook chapter by chapter instead of asking which of those chapters actually recur on real papers. The syllabus lists broad topic names; the exam itself tests a much narrower, more repetitive slice of them.

What actually shows up

Mechanics is the most reliable source of questions: kinematics (speed, acceleration, simple projectile setups), forces and Newton's laws, and work and energy, usually dressed up as a real-world scenario rather than a bare equation. Fluids show up often too, and disproportionately so given how little classroom time they usually get - pressure, density, and buoyancy, often phrased in a way that overlaps with biology (blood pressure, floating, syringes). Basic electricity appears regularly as well, almost always simple circuits and Ohm's law rather than anything more elaborate. Heat and thermodynamics recur in a limited form: specific heat, phase changes, and basic gas behavior, not full thermodynamic cycles.

What almost never shows up

Modern physics is essentially absent - no relativity, no quantum mechanics, no nuclear physics beyond the most surface-level fact recall. Electromagnetism rarely goes past a basic circuit; you won't be asked to analyze induction or magnetic fields in any depth. Optics appears only in its simplest form, if at all, and multi-step problems requiring several formulas chained together don't fit the exam's one-minute-per-question format anyway. If a chapter needs a page of derivation to explain, it's very unlikely to be the source of an IMAT question.

Why the overlap with biology matters

A lot of the physics that does appear isn't purely physics - it's physics wearing a lab coat. Fluid pressure and buoyancy questions often read like a biology scenario about blood flow or diffusion. That overlap is useful: time spent getting comfortable with pressure, concentration gradients, and basic rates pays off in both the physics questions and the biology data questions that lean on the same underlying logic.

How to actually prioritize your physics prep

Put your limited hours into mechanics, fluids, basic electricity, and simple thermodynamics, in roughly that order of return. Skip or skim modern physics and deep electromagnetism unless you're already confident in them for other reasons - that time moves your score further almost anywhere else. And practice recognizing the setup fast, since the actual computation in most physics questions is short once you know which two or three quantities are related.
