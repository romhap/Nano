What Student Clubs and Research Opportunities Actually Look Like in Italian Medical School

Maya Oct 5, 2026 · 4 min read

Most of what candidates hear about life after the IMAT is about lectures, exams, and surviving the transition. Almost nobody mentions that Italian medical schools also run a whole layer of student associations, volunteering groups, and early research opportunities sitting alongside the coursework. It's not required, and nobody will chase you to join, but it's there, and knowing roughly what it looks like before you arrive makes it much easier to actually use.

The student associations are real and mostly student-run

Nearly every Italian medical school has a local chapter of SISM, the national medical students' association tied to the international IFMSA network, running volunteering projects, health awareness campaigns, and exchange programs between member countries. Alongside it you'll typically find subject-specific societies, international student groups, and smaller faculty-run clubs, almost all organized and led by students a year or two ahead of you, not administration staff.

Research access starts earlier than most expect, but it's not handed to you

Shadowing a lab or getting onto a small research project is genuinely possible from early in the degree, several candidates we've worked with started in their first or second year, but it isn't something that gets assigned automatically. It almost always starts with directly emailing a professor whose area interests you and asking if there's room for a student, sometimes more than once, since timing and lab capacity matter as much as interest does.

None of it replaces the coursework, and timing matters

Associations and early research add real value, a sense of community, a line on a future CV, actual exposure to how medicine gets practiced, but they sit on top of a demanding course load, not instead of it. The students who get the most out of them tend to wait until the first semester's logistics and rhythm have settled before adding anything extra, rather than signing up for everything in orientation week and burning out by November.

What's actually worth doing about it

Treat your first weeks as a listening period: find out which associations and labs are actually active at your specific university, since this varies school to school, before committing to anything. Pick one thing that genuinely interests you rather than several that look good on paper, and give the coursework priority until its rhythm feels manageable. The opportunities aren't going anywhere in the first semester, and joining later from a stable footing beats joining early and dropping out by December.
