The IMAT's Genetics Questions Aren't a Memorization Exercise

Maya Oct 18, 2026 · 4 min read

No - IMAT genetics questions mostly test whether you can work through Mendelian ratios and pedigrees correctly, not whether you've memorized vocabulary. Genetics sits inside Biology's 23 of the IMAT's 60 questions, and real papers lean on probability and inheritance-pattern logic far more than on recalling terms.

Candidates treat genetics like a vocabulary list because the terms sound like a glossary: dominant, recessive, codominant, sex-linked, allele. So they flashcard the definitions, feel confident, and still miss questions on the actual paper. The gap isn't the definitions. It's that the exam uses those terms as the input to a calculation, not as the thing being tested.

Where genetics actually sits inside Biology

Biology is the largest section on the IMAT, and genetics and heredity is one of several themes that repeat inside it every year, alongside cell biology, molecular biology, metabolism, physiology, and evolution. It isn't a separate section with its own time allocation, it's a recurring slice of Biology's footprint, the same way biochemistry is. Treat it as one more theme to get efficient at, not an entire subject to master from a textbook.

Why it feels like a memorization section, and why that's the wrong prep

The terminology matters, you do need to know what "autosomal recessive" or "codominant" means before you can set a problem up. But knowing the definition gets you to the starting line, not the answer. A question that gives you two parents' genotypes and asks for the probability of a specific offspring phenotype isn't rewarding the fact that you know what "heterozygous" means, it's rewarding whether you can build the cross correctly and read the ratio off it under time pressure.

What actually gets asked

Two question types do most of the work on real papers. The first is a direct Punnett square problem: given parent genotypes or phenotypes, predict the ratio of outcomes in their offspring, sometimes for a single trait, sometimes for two at once. The second is pedigree analysis: given a family tree showing who has a trait and who doesn't, work out the most likely inheritance pattern (dominant, recessive, or sex-linked) and then the probability for a specific person in that tree. Basic allele frequency questions show up too, but less often, and at a simple, conceptual level rather than anything requiring a memorized formula.

The skill underneath it is probability, not recall

Once the genotypes are set up correctly, the math itself is simple ratios, 3:1 for a basic monohybrid cross, 9:3:3:1 for a dihybrid one. The exam isn't testing whether you can do that arithmetic, almost everyone can. It's testing whether you set the cross up right in the first place, under time pressure, without a diagram already drawn for you. That's a setup skill, and setup skills get better with repetition on real problems, not with more flashcards of terms you already recognize.

How to actually train this

Work real Punnett square and pedigree problems from past papers, repeatedly, until building the cross or reading the tree becomes close to automatic. When you get one wrong, check whether it was a setup error (wrong cross, misread pedigree symbol) before assuming it was a content gap, because it usually is a setup error, and those two problems get fixed in completely different ways. A short list of the specific setup mistakes you keep making, revisited often, will move your score more than another pass through a genetics chapter.
