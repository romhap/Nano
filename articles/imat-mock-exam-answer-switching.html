Changing Your Answer on an IMAT Mock: When It Helps, When It Hurts

Rom Oct 25, 2026 · 4 min read

Change your answer when you have a specific reason: you realize you misread the question, remembered something new, or spotted a clue elsewhere on the paper. Don't change it just because a second look makes you suddenly unsure. That kind of switch, driven by doubt alone rather than a real reason, is the one most likely to turn a right answer into a wrong one.

"Trust your first instinct" is repeated so often during mock exam debriefs that it's become a rule nobody questions. It's not a bad instinct to have, but it's the wrong level of advice. The question isn't whether to trust your first answer in general, it's whether the specific thing that's making you reconsider is a real signal or just nerves.

Why "trust your first instinct" is too blunt a rule

First answers are often right because your initial read of a question, when you're not yet second-guessing it, tends to be your clearest read. But first answers are also often wrong for the exact same reason, you can misread a question cleanly and confidently on the first pass and never notice. A blanket rule to never switch protects you from panic-driven changes, but it also locks in misreads you'd have caught on a second look. The rule that actually works has to look at why you're reconsidering, not just whether you are.

The two reasons people switch, and why only one of them works

There are two very different things happening when you hover over a different option. The first is that something concrete triggered the doubt: you reread the question stem and noticed a word you missed the first time, you got to a later question that reminded you of a fact relevant to this one, or you worked the problem a second way and got a different result. That's new information, and acting on new information is exactly what you should do. The second is that you've simply stared at the question too long and your confidence eroded, with nothing new actually entering the picture. That's not information, it's fatigue, and switching on fatigue alone removes a correct answer roughly as often as it fixes a wrong one.

Where this runs into the IMAT's scoring formula

This matters more on the IMAT specifically because of how the exam is scored: wrong answers cost you points and blanks don't (see the IMAT's scoring trick). That means a switch between two options you're torn on, with no clean reason either way, isn't risk-free just because you're "deciding between two answers" rather than guessing cold. If you don't have a specific reason to move off your first pick, the safer default is to leave it and move on, not to keep negotiating with yourself.

How to actually catch this pattern in your mocks

After a mock, go back through every question where you remember hesitating or changing your answer, and sort them into two piles: switches with a specific reason, and switches made from doubt alone. Most candidates have never looked at this split before, and it's often the single clearest pattern in their error log. If your reasoned switches are helping and your doubt-driven switches are hurting, which is the common pattern, you now have a concrete rule for next time: only move off a first answer when you can name the reason.

Building this into how you review

This only becomes useful if you actually track it, which means it belongs in how you review a mock exam, not just how you take one. A single mock won't give you a reliable pattern, but three or four reviewed this way usually will, and once you see your own switching tendency clearly, it stops being a vague anxiety and becomes a rule you can follow on the real exam.
