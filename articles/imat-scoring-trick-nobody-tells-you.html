The IMAT Scoring Trick Nobody Tells You About

Rom Aug 20, 2026 · 4 min read

Most candidates treat the IMAT like a normal test: answer everything, hope for the best, move on. That instinct is the fastest way to lose points you'd already earned — because the IMAT isn't scored like a normal test.

The formula everyone glances at and nobody actually uses

Every correct answer is worth +1.5. Every wrong answer costs you -0.4. A blank costs nothing. Max score is 90. Everyone knows these numbers. Almost nobody sits down and does the actual math on what they mean for how you should answer a question you're not sure about.

Here's the math. If you guess completely blind among all five options, your expected value per question is:

(1/5 × 1.5) − (4/5 × 0.4) = 0.30 − 0.32 = −0.02

Basically break-even, slightly negative. That's why "just guess, you might get lucky" is bad advice on its own. But watch what happens the moment you can eliminate even one option — not the whole thing, just one you're confident is wrong:

(1/4 × 1.5) − (3/4 × 0.4) = 0.375 − 0.30 = +0.075

And with two eliminated, it climbs further:

(1/3 × 1.5) − (2/3 × 0.4) = 0.50 − 0.267 = +0.233

That's the trick. You don't need certainty to make guessing worth it. You need one plausible option gone. The exam isn't testing whether you know every answer cold — it's testing whether you can tell a plausible option from a planted one, fast.

Why this should change how you study

If elimination is worth more than raw recall, then drilling "do I know this fact or not" is only half the job. The other half — the half almost nobody trains on purpose — is pattern-recognition on distractors: spotting units that don't match, a sign flip, an answer that's too round to be a real calculated value, a definition that's almost right but describes a different structure entirely.

Real IMAT wrong answers aren't random. They're built from common misconceptions and one-step-off calculations. Once you've seen enough real questions, you start recognizing the shape of a wrong answer before you've fully worked out the right one. That skill is trainable, and it's worth more exam points per hour of practice than memorizing one more obscure fact.

The 100-minute trap

60 questions, 100 minutes — just under 100 seconds a question on average. But the sections aren't equal weight or equal difficulty, and neither is your own strength across them. Spending four minutes fighting one biology question while an easy 20-second physics formula sits unanswered later in the paper is how strong candidates finish under their real ceiling.

A simple rule: hit your strongest section first. Bank the points you're certain of while your head is clearest, flag anything that's taking too long, and come back to it with whatever time is left. Never let one hard question eat time that ten easy ones downstream are waiting for.
