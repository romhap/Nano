After the IMAT

How Exam Sessions Actually Work in Italian Medical School

Maya Sep 25, 2026 · 4 min read

The IMAT trains you for one date. Everything is building toward a single Tuesday in September, and once it's over, it's over. Enrolled students expect medical school to work the same way, one exam period, one shot per subject, and then get genuinely thrown by how different the actual structure is.

There's no single exam period, there are several

Italian universities run exams in blocks called appelli, spread across the year rather than clustered at the end of a semester. A single subject might be examinable across two or three separate sessions within a few months, sometimes more. Instead of one fixed date you either hit or miss, you're choosing from a small set of windows, and that choice is entirely yours to make.

Choosing when to sit an exam is a real decision

Being technically eligible to sit an appello doesn't mean you have to take it. Plenty of students let the first available session for a hard subject pass on purpose, using the extra weeks to prepare properly rather than sitting underprepared just because the date was open. The tradeoff is real too: pushing everything to the last possible session in a block stacks several exams close together, which creates its own kind of pressure.

What actually happens if you don't pass, or don't sit

Skipping an appello you're not ready for carries no penalty beyond waiting for the next one. Failing one is similar, there's no permanent mark against you, just another attempt at a later session, sometimes as soon as the next available date. What does matter is the degree program's own internal deadlines for finishing each year's coursework, which is a separate structure from the appelli themselves and worth understanding early rather than discovering by accident.

How this changes the rhythm of your year

The IMAT rewards peaking on one date. Medical school in Italy rewards pacing several subjects across a spread of windows, some taken early, some deliberately delayed, based on an honest read of your own readiness rather than a fixed calendar. That's a genuinely different skill from exam-day intensity, and it's worth expecting the adjustment rather than being caught off guard by it in your first term.
