What Actually Happens When You Try to Find Student Housing in Italy

Rom Sep 8, 2026 · 4 min read

Candidates spend months ranking universities by score cutoffs, reputation, and city size, and then treat housing as something to sort out after they arrive. That order is backwards. Where you'll actually sleep, cook, and study affects your first year more than almost anything else on the list, and it's the part most people research least.

The three options you're actually choosing between

University-run student residences (often called collegi) are the cheapest and most structured option, but spots are limited and usually allocated by merit or income, not just by applying early. Private rentals give you the most control but require a local guarantor in a lot of cases, which is a real problem if you don't already know anyone in the city. Shared apartments found through other students, usually via Facebook groups or word of mouth once you're enrolled, tend to be the realistic middle ground for most people.

Why timing matters more than people expect

The Italian rental market moves on its own calendar, tied to the academic year, not to when IMAT results come out. Good listings for September get snapped up over the summer, right around when a lot of candidates are still waiting to hear which university they've been placed at. That gap between "you have your ranking" and "you have a lease" is where most of the stress actually happens.

What it actually costs, city to city

A room in Milan or Rome can run two to three times what the same room costs in a smaller university town. That difference compounds over six years, and it's worth factoring into your list alongside the usual things like class size or teaching hospital access, not as an afterthought once you've already committed.

How to start before you're already there

Reach out to current students at any university you're seriously considering, before results even come out. Ask specifically about collegio waitlists and typical rent, not just general "what's it like" questions. Having a rough plan for two or three of your realistic outcomes beats scrambling for one after the fact.

Not sure how much weight to give practical factors like housing when you're still ranking your university choices? That's exactly the kind of decision 1:1 mentorship helps you work through, weighing it against your realistic score and the rest of your list before you submit.
